# Batch 1 · Timur — Profile Draft

Nov 13, 2026 · @Alex Dowie

Timur set out to rebuild the Mongol Empire without being descended from Genghis Khan, and came close: from the 1370s to his death in 1405 he campaigned from Delhi to Ankara and from Damascus to the steppes north of the Caspian. He is remembered for the destruction of cities and for the culture his dynasty created in Samarkand and Herat. This profile tests how the database handles a conqueror who manufactured his own legitimacy, down to (probably) his birth date.

### A test case: the invented birthday

Timurid histories give his birth year as 1336 (in the Islamic calendar, 736). Manz suspects this was invented to coincide with the death of Abu Sa'id, the last Mongol ruler of Iran, presenting Timur as the Ilkhans' successor. Her reasons:

- The two official histories written during his life give no birth date at all.
- Ibn Arabshah says he was nearly eighty at his death, pointing to the 1320s.
- Jean of Sultaniyya put him at about seventy-five in 1403, and Mignanelli at seventy-four in 1400–01.
- Only Ibn Khaldun's estimate (sixty to seventy in 1401) fits 1336, and he gives no date.
- The one dated mention of 1336 from his lifetime is a marginal note in an endowment deed from the late 1390s.

(Manz 1988, note 33; B as her interpretation.) Britannica still gives 1336 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Timur)). The database stores both claims and grades the birth year C. It is a textbook example of a "fact" created for political reasons.

### The skeleton

In 1941 Soviet archaeologists opened his tomb in Samarkand and found the skeleton of a powerfully built man of above-average height, lame on the right side (Britannica, A). This confirms the injuries behind the name "Timur the Lame".

## Life and historical context

### Origins

Timur belonged to the Barlas, a Turkicised Mongol tribe that had settled in Transoxania (roughly modern Uzbekistan) after taking part in the campaigns of Genghis Khan's son Chagatai. He grew up in the Chagatai khanate, the Mongol successor state of Central Asia ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Timur), A). By his time it was a mosaic of tribes, settled regions and independent military bands with competing leaders (Manz's book, via a review excerpt; B).

### Rise to power (1360s–1370)

After the death of the region's ruler, Amir Kazgan, in 1357, Timur first declared loyalty to Tughluq Temür, the Chagatai khan of the east, who seized Samarkand in 1361 and made Timur a minister to his son. Timur soon broke away and joined his brother-in-law Amir Husayn; together they defeated the khan's son in 1364 and won control of Transoxania by about 1366. Around 1370 Timur turned on Husayn, besieged him at Balkh, and after Husayn was assassinated, had himself proclaimed ruler at Samarkand (Britannica, A for the outline).

### The legitimacy problem

Timur was not a descendant of Genghis Khan, and by long steppe convention only Genghis Khan's descendants could be khan. Manz shows that he worked within severe restraints, from both the Turco-Mongol and the Islamic traditions. His own formulation of his legitimacy was later built up by his successors into an elaborate myth linking his dynasty to Genghis Khan's ([Manz 1988](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/S0021086200014924/type/journal_article), A). In practice he ruled as amir (commander), kept a Chinggisid figurehead khan, and through marriage to a Chinggisid princess took the title küregen, "royal son-in-law" (B, from Manz's notes and other excerpts).

### Context: a broken Mongol world

By the 1360s the Mongol successor states had collapsed or split. The Ilkhanate in Iran had dissolved after the death of its last effective ruler in 1335, leaving rival dynasties unable to resist jointly (Britannica, A); the Mongols had lost China to the Ming in 1368; and the Golden Horde in the west was divided. Timur's project, Manz writes, was to conquer the whole of the former Mongol Empire, and he almost succeeded (Manz 1988, A as her judgement).

## Military career

Unless marked, events follow [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Timur), checked against Manz where she covers them.

**Central Asia (1370s).** About ten years of war against the eastern Chagatai khans and Khwarazm, ending with the occupation of Kashgar in 1380 (A).

**Persia (1380s–1394).** He began the conquest of Persia with Herat (1383 in Britannica; other accounts say 1381, so the year is C). Eastern Persia fell by 1385; Fars, Iraq, Azerbaijan, Armenia, Mesopotamia and Georgia between 1386 and 1394. Revolts behind his lines were crushed: Britannica records whole cities destroyed, populations massacred and towers built of their skulls (A for the practice). Isfahan in 1387 is the best-known case (B, to source).

**Tokhtamysh and the Golden Horde (1385–1395).** Tokhtamysh, a Chinggisid prince Timur had once sheltered and helped to power, became khan of the Golden Horde and turned against him, invading Azerbaijan in 1385 and Transoxania in 1388. In 1391 Timur pursued him deep into the steppe and defeated him; in 1395, after a new invasion, he defeated him decisively and then devastated the Horde's lands (A for the outline). Two details in Britannica conflict with standard accounts and are stored as disputed (C): it places the 1395 battle on the Kur River, where most accounts say the Terek; and it says Timur occupied Moscow for a year, whereas standard accounts have him turning back south before reaching Moscow.

**India (1398–99).** He invaded the Delhi Sultanate on the pretext that its Muslim rulers were too tolerant of their Hindu subjects, crossed the Indus in September 1398, destroyed the Delhi sultan's army on 17 December and left Delhi in ruins; Britannica says it took more than a century to recover. He was back in Samarkand by April 1399 with enormous spoils; Clavijo reports 90 captured elephants hauling stone for a mosque there (A for the campaign; B for the elephants). Britannica places the battle at Panipat, while other accounts place it outside Delhi itself (C for the site).

**The Mamluks and Ottomans (1399–1402).** He stormed and sacked Aleppo, defeated the Mamluk army, and occupied Damascus in 1401, deporting its artisans to Samarkand. Outside Damascus, the historian Ibn Khaldun was brought to meet him and later wrote an account of their conversations (B, via Manz's citations). Baghdad was stormed the same year; Britannica reports 20,000 inhabitants massacred (B, `inflated-numbers` pending a scholarly figure). In July 1402 he destroyed the army of the Ottoman sultan Bayezid I near Ankara and took Bayezid prisoner (A), then captured Smyrna from the Knights of Rhodes.

**China and death (1404–05).** Back in Samarkand in 1404, he prepared an invasion of Ming China, set out at the end of December, fell ill at Otrar on the Syr Darya, and died in February 1405 (A). He was buried in the Gur-e Amir in Samarkand.

## Leadership, strategy and the Timurid state

### Leadership

- **Always in the field.** He personally led forces that were almost constantly on campaign, never took up a permanent residence, and when he visited his magnificent capital, Samarkand, stayed only a few days before returning to his encampment ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Timur), A).
- **Personal loyalty as the system.** Manz's study shows how he made personal loyalty the test, balancing his own control against the demands of tribes and clans (Manz's book, via review excerpts; B). The weakness of this system showed at once: he left no orderly way of choosing a successor, and his sons and grandsons fought over the empire after his death (Britannica, A).
- **Legitimacy as a craft.** Without Chinggisid descent, he built his authority from Turco-Mongol, Islamic and Persian symbols together (Manz 1988, A).

### Strategy

- **Exploit every weakness.** Britannica describes him as a master of the military techniques developed under Genghis Khan, who used intrigue, treachery and alliance as readily as battle, sent agents to undermine enemies before an engagement, and conducted diplomacy recorded in archives from England to China (A as a summary judgement).
- **Terror as policy.** The massacres and skull towers that followed rebellions were meant to deter the next revolt (A for the practice; interpretation B).
- **Conquest without holding.** Manz notes that although he defeated both the Ottoman sultan and Tokhtamysh, he set up lasting administration only over the former Ilkhanate lands and the western Chagatai lands (Manz, excerpt; B). India, Syria, Anatolia and the Golden Horde were raided, broken and left.
- **Wealth to Samarkand.** Deporting skilled artisans from conquered cities (Damascus, Delhi and others) to build and adorn Samarkand turned plunder into a capital (Britannica, A).

### Tactics and technology

- **Mounted archers and swordsmen,** with mobility and surprise as the main weapons of attack (Britannica, A).
- **Siege warfare** against the great walled cities of Iran, Syria and Iraq (A from results).
- **Captured war elephants** from India, later used as labour in Samarkand (Clavijo, via Britannica; B).

### The Timurid state

His lasting creations were cultural more than political. Samarkand's mosques and his own tomb, the Gur-e Amir, are among the masterpieces of Islamic architecture. Under his descendants the Timurid dynasty became famous for science (his grandson Ulugh Beg's observatory in Samarkand) and for the Persian miniature painting of Herat (Britannica, A).

## Controversies and perspectives

### Mass killing

Britannica opens by saying he is chiefly remembered for the barbarity of his conquests, and records cities destroyed, populations massacred and towers built of skulls, Delhi left in ruins for more than a century, and a massacre at Baghdad (A for the practice). Specific death tolls in the sources come mostly from victims and hostile writers and are flagged `inflated-numbers`. Modern totals in the millions that circulate online are estimates built on those figures and are not stored as facts (D).

### Religion as pretext

He was a Muslim ruler whose victims were mostly Muslims: the dynasties of Iran, the Delhi Sultanate, the Mamluks of Syria and the Ottomans. He justified the invasion of India by accusing its Muslim sultans of excessive tolerance toward Hindus (Britannica, A), and presented the capture of Christian-held Smyrna as holy war. The database records these as stated justifications, not motives (C for any claim about his true motives).

### The Uzbek claim

Celebrating Timur as the founder of the Uzbek nation is anachronistic in one respect: the Uzbeks as a political people take their name from a later Golden Horde ruler, and Uzbek confederations drove Timur's own descendants out of Central Asia in the early 16th century. The claim is recorded with `nationalist-reinterpretation`, alongside the fact that it is held (C for the framing).

## Legacy and myths

### Legacy

- **The last great steppe conqueror.** Britannica calls him the last of the mighty Central Asian conquerors to rule both farming and herding peoples on an imperial scale (A as judgement).
- **A dynasty of culture.** The Timurid renaissance of the 15th century: Ulugh Beg's observatory in Samarkand, the miniature painters of Herat, and the architecture of Samarkand (Britannica, A).
- **The Mughals.** His descendant Babur, driven from Central Asia, founded the Mughal dynasty of India in the early 16th century (Britannica, A). The Mughals' name itself comes from the Persian word for Mongol (May, A).
- **Interrupting the Ottomans.** The defeat at Ankara threw the young Ottoman state into a succession crisis before it recovered and took Constantinople in 1453 (B, to source).
- **In European imagination,** the destruction he caused gave rise to legends, and to Christopher Marlowe's play *Tamburlaine the Great* (Britannica, A).

## Learning aids

### Key takeaways

1. Timur, a Turkicised Mongol of the Barlas tribe, rose to rule Transoxania around 1370 and spent the rest of his life at war.
2. He conquered Iran, broke the Golden Horde, sacked Delhi, Aleppo, Damascus and Baghdad, and captured the Ottoman sultan at Ankara in 1402.
3. Not a descendant of Genghis Khan, he built legitimacy from Mongol, Islamic and Persian symbols; even his traditional birth year may have been chosen for political effect.
4. He died in 1405 at the start of an invasion of Ming China; his empire fragmented, but his dynasty created the Timurid renaissance and, through Babur, the Mughal empire.
5. His campaigns caused mass killing, but the figures that circulate today are largely inflated.

### Compare with

- **Genghis Khan:** the model Timur set out to emulate, with the legitimacy Timur never had.
- **Baibars:** a later Mamluk sultan lost Syria's great cities to Timur, the state Baibars built a century earlier.
- **Babur:** his descendant, who carried the Timurid name to India.
