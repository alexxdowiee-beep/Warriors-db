# PER-000005 · Te Rauparaha — Pilot Profile

Nov 12, 2026 · @Alex Dowie

Te Rauparaha led Ngāti Toa from near-destruction at Kāwhia to the conquest of much of central New Zealand, and composed the haka Ka Mate. He is remembered very differently by Ngāti Toa, by the iwi his forces defeated, and by colonial writers, so this profile tests how the database holds several legitimate perspectives at once.

**Research note.** I opened two sources: Steven Oliver's biography of Te Rauparaha in the *Dictionary of New Zealand Biography* (1990, on Te Ara), and the Haka Ka Mate Attribution Act 2014, whose schedule sets out Ngāti Toa's own account of his birth and of the composition of Ka Mate. Older encyclopedia entries are cited from search excerpts. The perspectives of the iwi on the other side of his wars (Ngāi Tahu, Muaūpoko, Rangitāne, Ngāti Apa, Ngāti Kuia and others) are not yet represented by their own sources; that is the largest gap in this draft.

## The evidence

Te Rauparaha lived at the meeting point of two record-keeping systems. Before about 1827, nearly everything comes from Māori oral history; after European ships began trading at Kāpiti, missionaries, traders, officials and newspapers add written accounts, nearly all from outsiders.

### 1. Māori oral history and whakapapa

Ngāti Toa's own history, including whakapapa (genealogy), kōrero about the migration and the story of Ka Mate, is held and transmitted within the iwi. Part of it is now in the public record through the Treaty settlement: the 2014 Act sets out Ngāti Toa's account of his birth and of Ka Mate's composition in the iwi's own words ([Haka Ka Mate Attribution Act 2014](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2014/18/en/latest)). This is graded as tradition held by its rightful kaitiaki: A for what the iwi holds, and evaluated separately where it describes events.

The iwi defeated in his wars have their own oral histories, which often tell the same events very differently. A fair profile needs them alongside Ngāti Toa's.

### 2. His son's account

Tāmihana Te Rauparaha, his son, later wrote an account of his father's life (to locate and verify). It is the closest thing to an insider biography, and flagged `hagiography` as a son's record of a famous father.

### 3. Colonial writers

Nineteenth-century European writers often cast him as a cunning tyrant, a "Napoleon" of the south (label to verify against period sources), and dwelt on cannibalism and treachery. Their accounts are useful for events they witnessed, and flagged `hostile-source` and `popular-culture` for the image they built.

### 4. Modern scholarship

Steven Oliver's 1990 biography for the *Dictionary of New Zealand Biography* is the standard modern summary. It records events as remembered by several iwi, and ends with an important balance: the tribes he defeated attribute his success to muskets rather than to his genius, while without his leadership Ngāti Toa would probably not have attempted the migration at all ([DNZB](https://teara.govt.nz/en/biographies/1t74/te-rauparaha)).

## Life and historical context

### Name and early life

His name comes from rauparaha, an edible plant. Soon after his birth, a Waikato warrior who had killed and eaten one of his relations threatened to eat the child too, roasted with rauparaha leaves; the boy was named Te Rauparaha in defiance of the threat ([DNZB](https://teara.govt.nz/en/biographies/1t74/te-rauparaha), tradition, A as kōrero). As a child he was also called Māui Pōtiki, after the lively trickster of Māori tradition.

He spent much of his childhood with his mother's people at Maungatautari. Ngāti Toa's own account says he was raised as a rangatira and returned there to learn weaponry, the taiaha and the wahaika ([2014 Act](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2014/18/en/latest)). The DNZB notes that he was not of the highest rank by birth and rose to lead Ngāti Toa through his aggressive defence of the tribe's interests and his skill in war (B).

When the Ngāti Raukawa chief Hape-ki-tūārangi was dying and asked who would take his place, none of Hape's own sons answered and Te Rauparaha did; he later married Hape's widow, Te Ākau, mother of his son Tāmihana (DNZB, B as tradition).

### Kāwhia and the Waikato wars

From the late 18th century, Ngāti Toa and their relations were repeatedly at war with the Waikato iwi over the fertile land north of Kāwhia Harbour. The DNZB traces a long chain of killings, revenge raids and peacemakings, in which Te Rauparaha became a leading war leader and peace was sometimes negotiated through kinship, as when his sister Waitohi used her relations among the attackers to end a siege.

### The musket transforms war

Between about 1810 and 1815, while visiting Ngāti Maru in the Hauraki Gulf, Te Rauparaha received his first musket. In 1819 he joined a large northern war party armed with muskets, led by Ngāpuhi and Ngāti Whātua chiefs including Patuone and Nene, which swept south through Taranaki to Cook Strait; defenders who had never faced guns were demoralised (DNZB, A). On that expedition he saw a sailing ship in Cook Strait, probably from the Russian Bellingshausen expedition, and was told that a people settled near the strait could grow powerful by trading with passing ships (B).

This is the context for everything that followed: the musket, unevenly spread between iwi, turned long-running tribal conflicts into a decades-long upheaval now called the Musket Wars.

## Military career

All events below are from the DNZB unless marked. Violence against people on the other side is recorded plainly, because it is central to how those iwi remember him.

**Leaving Kāwhia (1820–21).** Several thousand Waikato and Ngāti Maniapoto warriors invaded Kāwhia. After defeats at Te Kakara and the loss of Waikawau pā, Ngāti Toa were besieged at Te Arawī. Kin among the besiegers helped them: one Ngāti Maniapoto leader secretly supplied food, and Ngāti Toa were allowed to leave. Te Rauparaha burned his carved house, recited a lament for Kāwhia and began the first stage of the migration, Te Heke Tahutahuahi. Shielding the women, children and wounded, he used deception: dressing people in red to suggest a Ngāpuhi war party nearby, and lighting many fires with cloaks spread over bushes to suggest a large army. About 1,500 Ngāti Toa reached Taranaki, where Te Āti Awa allowed them to cultivate land.

**Motunui (late 1821 or early 1822).** A pursuing Waikato force under Te Wherowhero was defeated, freeing Ngāti Toa from pursuit.

**The march to Kāpiti (1822).** The second stage, Te Heke Tātaramoa, joined by some Te Āti Awa, travelled about 250 miles south. It began peacefully through marriage alliances, but turned violent in Manawatū and Horowhenua. At a feast near Lake Papaitonga, Muaūpoko tried to kill Te Rauparaha and his family; he escaped, but several of his children were killed. In revenge, Muaūpoko's lake pā were taken and their people massacred. Te Pēhi Kupe, Ngāti Toa's senior chief, seized Kāpiti Island, which became their stronghold. Rangitāne people were killed at Hotuiti after a false offer of peace.

**Waiorua (c. 1824).** A great canoe fleet from many southern iwi, from Taranaki to the South Island, made a night attack on Kāpiti and was defeated. This secured Ngāti Toa in the south of the North Island.

**The South Island (c. 1827–1833).** Seeking greenstone and control over the region, he crossed Cook Strait. Rangitāne pā at Wairau were taken; at Hikapu in Pelorus Sound his force of about 340, mostly armed with guns, heavily defeated Ngāti Kuia; at Kaikōura many Ngāi Tahu were killed or enslaved. At Kaiapoi, Ngāi Tahu killed Te Pēhi Kupe and seven other Ngāti Toa chiefs who had entered the pā to trade, and the first attack failed.

**The *Elizabeth* (1830).** Captain John Stewart, a British trader, carried about 100 Ngāti Toa warriors hidden aboard his brig to Akaroa and lured the Ngāi Tahu chief Tama-i-hara-nui aboard. He was captured with his wife and daughter, and later tortured and put to death at Kāpiti; on the ship, he strangled his daughter to prevent her enslavement.

**Kaiapoi and Ōnawe (1831–32).** Kaiapoi pā was taken after a long siege by sapping and burning the palisades, and Ōnawe at Akaroa was then captured.

**Limits (1833–1839).** Ngāi Tahu, now armed from whaling stations, nearly captured him at Kaparatehau (Lake Grassmere), and later battles were inconclusive. Fighting among his own allies (Ngāti Raukawa and Te Āti Awa) twice threatened him, ending with Ngāti Raukawa's defeat at Te Kūititanga in October 1839, from which he escaped by whaleboat.

## Leadership, strategy and technology

### Leadership

- **Holding a coalition of migrants together.** Ngāti Toa were joined over the following decade by Ngāti Raukawa, Te Āti Awa, Ngāti Tama and others, who were given land in conquered territory. His power depended on keeping these allies supplied and settling disputes among them, and it was repeatedly strained when they fought each other (DNZB, A).
- **Kinship and marriage diplomacy.** Alliances made through marriage let the migration pass peacefully through some territories, and kin among enemies saved Ngāti Toa at Te Arawī (A).
- **Personal courage and escape.** He survived assassination at Papaitonga, near-capture at Lake Grassmere and defeat at Te Kūititanga (B).
- **Restraint when it counted.** After Wairau in 1843 he avoided war with the settlers, which the DNZB credits with contributing greatly to the crisis ending peacefully (A as the biographer's judgement).

### Strategy

- **Move the people, not just the army.** The migration relocated an entire iwi to a place chosen for its access to passing European ships and their trade in guns (A).
- **An island base.** Kāpiti gave Ngāti Toa security from mainland enemies and control of a busy anchorage (A).
- **Control the musket trade.** His power over allied tribes rested on controlling the trade in arms and ammunition. Captives were set to scraping flax, which was traded for muskets, powder and tobacco (DNZB, A).
- **Strike before the enemy is armed.** The South Island was open to conquest because its iwi had not yet acquired guns; once Ngāi Tahu armed themselves, his expansion stalled (A).

### Tactics

- **Deception and ruses:** the red cloth and false campfires during the escape from Kāwhia (B).
- **False peace offers,** such as at Hotuiti and reportedly at Pukerua. These are recorded as tactics, and are central to how the iwi affected remember him (B).
- **Siegecraft:** sapping and firing the palisades to take Kaiapoi (A).
- **Amphibious operations:** canoe fleets across Cook Strait, and in 1830 a European trading ship used to carry a hidden war party (A).

### Weapons and technology

- **Muskets,** the decisive technology of the period, unevenly spread between iwi.
- **Traditional weapons:** the taiaha and wahaika, in which Ngāti Toa's account says he was trained.
- **Waka (canoes) and later European whaleboats** for movement along the coast and across the strait.
- **Pā fortifications:** he built several on Kāpiti and, in the 1840s, on the mainland near Porirua.

## Politics, the Crown and controversies

### Land, the Treaty and Wairau

On 16 October 1839, the day of Te Kūititanga, the New Zealand Company ship *Tory* arrived at Kāpiti. Te Rauparaha accepted guns, blankets and other goods from William Wakefield for land whose extent was soon disputed; he insisted he had sold only Whakatū and Te Taitapu, in the Nelson and Golden Bay areas. Hobson's government declared such sales void pending investigation.

He signed the Treaty of Waitangi twice: on 14 May 1840, a copy brought by the missionary Henry Williams, and again on 19 June when Major Thomas Bunbury insisted. The DNZB says he believed the Treaty would guarantee him and his allies the territories won by conquest (A for the signings; B for his understanding).

In 1843 he and Te Rangihaeata stopped the survey of the Wairau plains. Arthur Wakefield led armed settlers from Nelson to arrest him. In the fighting, Te Rongo, Te Rangihaeata's wife, was killed; after the settlers surrendered, Te Rangihaeata killed them in revenge. Governor FitzRoy later found that the settlers had provoked the clash, and took no further action (DNZB, A).

### Arrest and detention

In 1846, amid fighting in the Hutt Valley, Governor George Grey decided Te Rauparaha could not be trusted. British troops seized him before dawn at his pā near Porirua. He was held without charge aboard HMS *Calliope* for ten months, then allowed to live in Auckland, and returned to Ōtaki in 1848 (DNZB, A). The DNZB notes his influence seems to have declined afterwards, possibly because of the humiliation.

### Death

He died at Ōtaki on 27 November 1849 and was buried near Rangiātea church; he is believed to have been reinterred on Kāpiti. He attended church services but did not become a Christian.

## Legacy and myths

### Ka Mate

Ngāti Toa's account, set out in the 2014 Act, is that Te Rauparaha composed Ka Mate while Ngāti Toa were still based at Kāwhia. Pursued by a Ngāti Te Aho war party, he sought help from Mananui Te Heuheu of Ngāti Tūwharetoa, who sent him to Te Wharerangi's pā on an island in Lake Rotoaira. Te Wharerangi hid him in a food pit with his wife, Te Rangikoaea, sitting at its entrance; her presence protected him physically and spiritually, blocking the enemy tohunga's karakia. The haka's famous lines move between "Will I die?" and "Will I live?" as the danger rose and fell, and end with his emergence into the sunlight ([2014 Act](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2014/18/en/latest), A as Ngāti Toa's account).

The Act records Ka Mate as a taonga of Ngāti Toa Rangatira, who are its kaitiaki. In Ngāti Toa's words, such a composition is not the composer's own property but a treasure of his iwi. The Act requires commercial and public uses to credit Te Rauparaha as composer and a chief of Ngāti Toa; ordinary performances, including kapa haka, are exempt (A).

### Wider legacy

- **The tribal map.** The DNZB concludes that, by taking his people from defeat at Kāwhia to conquest in central New Zealand, he changed the tribal structure of New Zealand for ever (A as the biographer's judgement).
- **Ngāti Toa Rangatira today,** based around Porirua and Te Tau Ihu (the top of the South Island), settled its historical Treaty claims with the Crown in 2014 (B, search excerpts).
- **His son Tāmihana** became a Christian evangelist who helped bring the fighting to an end in the south (DNZB, B).

## Learning aids

### Key takeaways

1. Driven from Kāwhia by Waikato in 1820–21, Te Rauparaha led Ngāti Toa and allied iwi south to Kāpiti and conquered much of the southern North Island and northern South Island.
2. Muskets, trade with European ships, kinship alliances and ruthless tactics all played a part; how much credit belongs to him rather than to muskets is debated.
3. His wars brought massacre and enslavement to several iwi, who remember him very differently from Ngāti Toa.
4. He signed the Treaty of Waitangi twice, was detained without charge by Governor Grey in 1846–47, and died at Ōtaki in 1849.
5. He composed Ka Mate, a Ngāti Toa taonga whose authorship is now recognised in law.

### Compare with

- **Shaka:** another early 19th-century leader whose wars reshaped his region, and whose reputation was heavily shaped by colonial writers.
- **Kamehameha I:** a Pacific leader who used European weapons and ships to build power in the same decades.
- **Sundiata Keita:** both are known largely through the oral traditions of their own people, set against outside written sources.

### Pilot notes for this profile

- **Add `interpretation` to `claim_type`.** Claims such as "his power rested on the musket trade" are neither events nor tradition; they are a historian's judgement and should be graded as such.
- **Add a `perspective_of` field to claims.** It links a claim to the people whose view it records (Ngāti Toa, Ngāi Tahu, Muaūpoko and so on), so the app can show a battle from each side instead of one blended narrative.
- **Add a `cultural_guidance` note to records.** Taonga such as Ka Mate, and knowledge held by iwi as kaitiaki, should carry a note on appropriate use, for example not reproducing the haka's words in quizzes or games. This will also matter for sacred material from other cultures on the list.
