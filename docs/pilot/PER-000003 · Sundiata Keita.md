# PER-000003 · Sundiata Keita — Pilot Profile

Nov 12, 2026 · @Alex Dowie

Sundiata founded the Mali Empire in the 13th century, but almost everything about him comes from the oral epic performed by Mande griots and recorded only in the 20th century. This profile tests whether the database can respect oral tradition as evidence without treating its every episode as fact.

## The evidence problem

For Sundiata, the question is not "which source is biased?" but "what kind of truth does each source hold?" There are three streams of evidence, and they are not independent of each other.

### 1. The oral epic

The story of Sundiata is still performed by jeli (griots), hereditary specialists in history, genealogy and music whose praise songs remain part of weddings and national holidays ([Met Museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-empires-of-the-western-sudan-mali-empire)). The epic is a living performance, not a fixed text. Each version is shaped by the performer, the audience and the family being praised.

The best-known version in English, D. T. Niane's *Sundiata: An Epic of Old Mali* (French 1960, English 1965), is a literary reworking of tales told by the griot Mamadou Kouyaté, not a word-for-word transcript (search excerpt; to verify against Niane's preface).

### 2. Arabic written sources

Ibn Khaldun, writing in North Africa in the late 14th century, more than a century after Sundiata, names a Mari Jata who conquered the Sosso and founded Mali's power. Ibn Battuta visited Mali in 1352–53. Both drew on Malian informants, so their information ultimately comes from Mande memory too. In the database, these sources will carry `derives_from` links to the oral tradition, so they are not counted as fully independent proof.

### 3. "Feedback"

Traffic also ran the other way. Bühnen notes that some recorded griot versions call the hero "Mari Jata", which he attributes to contamination from colonial-era writers who had read Ibn Khaldun (search excerpt of his article). He also points out that oral tradition is the *only* evidence that a Sosso empire existed at all ([Bühnen 1994](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/history-in-africa/article/abs/in-quest-of-susu/2ECEF02ECCFB74B2547F582D13C6DE21)).

### How this profile grades

Each claim from the epic is stored twice where needed:

- **As tradition content:** "the epic says X" — usually grade A, because we can verify what the tradition says.
- **As a historical event:** "X happened" — graded on whether anything outside the epic supports it.

Oral tradition is not downgraded for being oral. It is strong evidence for the broad shape of events remembered across many communities (a founder, a great war, a new political order) and weak evidence for dates, numbers, speeches and miracles.

## Life and historical context

### Names

"Sundiata" or "Sunjata" is usually explained as a fusion of his mother's name, Sogolon, and *jata*, "lion". Ibn Khaldun's "Mari Jata" uses *mari*, a royal title. "Keita" is a clan name, and scholars disagree about which family name he himself bore (C).

### Birth, childhood and exile (tradition)

In the epic, Sundiata is the son of a Mande king and Sogolon, a woman foretold to bear a great ruler. He cannot walk as a child and is mocked, until he suddenly rises and becomes strong. Driven out by a jealous co-wife and her son, he goes into exile with his mother and siblings, finally finding refuge in the kingdom of Mema.

These episodes are graded **A as tradition content** but **E as literal events**: they follow patterns common to founder stories (the despised child, the miraculous rise, the exile and return) and have no outside support. An exile before his return is plausible in outline (C).

### Historical context

The old Soninke empire of Ghana had declined by the 12th century. In its wake the Soso (Sosso), under Sumanguru Kanté, dominated the region, including the Mande chiefdoms of the upper Niger ([Met Museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-empires-of-the-western-sudan-mali-empire)). Power in the western Sudan rested on controlling the trans-Saharan trade in gold and salt, and on cavalry, because horses were costly and prized; the Met notes Sumanguru's cavalry in particular is remembered in both written and oral histories (search excerpt of [Met Museum, Western Sudan](https://www.metmuseum.org/es/essays/the-empires-of-the-western-sudan)).

### Death

The year c. 1255 is a modern estimate built from king-lists and reign lengths (C). Traditions disagree about how he died; versions include drowning in a river and an accidental arrow wound. None has outside support, so all are recorded as alternative tradition claims (D), not chosen between.

## Military career

Almost the whole of Sundiata's recorded military career is one war: a Mande revolt against Sosso rule, ending at Kirina. Both the oral tradition and Ibn Khaldun agree on that core, which is why it is the best-supported part of his story.

**The coalition.** In the epic, envoys from the Mande peoples find Sundiata in exile and ask him to return and lead resistance to Sumanguru. He comes back with troops from his hosts and forms an alliance of Mande clans and neighbouring rulers. A coalition of chiefdoms, rather than a single kingdom's army, is plausible and consistent with how Mali was later organised (B for a coalition, C for its named members).

**The war with Sumanguru.** The epic presents the struggle as a contest of power and magic as much as of arms. Sumanguru is a sorcerer-king who cannot be harmed by ordinary weapons; his secret is discovered and he is defeated. As tradition content this is A; as literal events it is E. The underlying history, a decisive victory over the Sosso ruler, is supported by both streams of evidence (B).

**Kirina (conventionally c. 1235).** The decisive battle took place at Kirina (Krina) on the upper Niger. The year 1235 is widely repeated, but it is a modern reconstruction from later king-lists, not a date stated in any medieval source (C, to verify who first proposed it). After the victory, Sumanguru disappears from the story and the Sosso capital is taken.

**Later expansion.** Traditions credit Sundiata's generals with further conquests, including a campaign to the west associated with his commander Tiramakhan Traoré. These are regional traditions with their own interests in claiming a link to the founder (D).

## Leadership, strategy and technology

For Sundiata, what can be said about leadership comes from the structure of what he built, not from eyewitness accounts. That makes this section thinner than for Joan or Hannibal, and that is the honest result.

### Leadership

- **Coalition-building.** His central achievement was persuading separate Mande chiefdoms and outside allies to fight under one leader, then keeping them together afterwards. This is the leadership quality best supported by the outcome itself (B).
- **The hunter-king image.** Tradition presents him as a great hunter, tied to the hunters' associations (donso) that were an important social and military force in Mande society. As an image, A; as a description of his personal skills, C.
- **Spiritual authority.** In the epic, victory depends on knowledge and ritual power as much as on soldiers. Whatever one makes of the magic, this shows how Mande audiences understood legitimate power: a ruler had to command both people and unseen forces (A as tradition content).

### Strategy and tactics

- **Turn subject peoples into allies.** The war is framed as liberation from Sosso rule, which drew allies who had their own reasons to fight.
- **Decisive battle.** Tradition concentrates the war into one great confrontation at Kirina, after which Sosso power collapses. Whether the real war was this decisive or a longer struggle is unknown (C).
- No specific battlefield tactics are attested in any source outside the epic, and none are invented here.

### Weapons and technology

- **Cavalry.** Across the western Sudan, horses were costly and owned only by the powerful, and they underpinned imperial control of trade routes (Met Museum, B for the region). Specific use of cavalry at Kirina is tradition only (C).
- **Archers and hunters' weapons,** bows above all, prominent in the hunter tradition (C).
- **Ironworking.** Sumanguru is remembered as a blacksmith-king, and blacksmiths held special, sometimes feared, status in Mande society. Iron weapons were central to the forces on both sides (B for ironworking's importance; C for any detail).

## Achievements and controversies

### Political achievements

- **Founding Mali.** The Met describes Sundiata's revolt against Sumanguru as marking the rise of the Mali empire, which later grew larger than western Europe and became famous under Mansa Musa ([Met Museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-empires-of-the-western-sudan-mali-empire), A for his founding role).
- **A federal structure.** Tradition describes Mali as an alliance of clans and kingdoms with defined rights and duties, not a single conquered territory. This fits how the empire later worked (B).
- **The Manden Charter (Kurukan Fuga).** See below.

### The Manden Charter

In 2009 UNESCO inscribed the Manden Charter on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. UNESCO describes it as proclaimed in the early 13th century by the empire's founder and an assembly of elders after a great victory, organised as a preamble and seven chapters covering social peace, the inviolability of the person, education, food security, the abolition of slavery by raiding, and freedom of expression and trade. It is still transmitted orally within Malinke clans, with annual ceremonies at Kangaba ([UNESCO](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/manden-charter-proclaimed-in-kouroukan-fouga-00290)).

### Controversies and open questions

- **Religion (C).** Tradition largely presents him as a hunter and master of traditional religion, while some of his family bore Muslim names and later Mali kings were Muslim. Whether he was a Muslim, nominally Muslim or not at all is disputed.
- **The title "mansa" (C).** Sources disagree on whether Sundiata himself used it or whether it became the royal title only after him.
- **Succession (C).** Oral traditions conflict with each other and with Ibn Khaldun about who ruled after him (search excerpt).
- **Slavery.** The Charter's ban on enslavement by raiding should not be read as ending slavery in Mali. Later travellers' accounts describe enslaved people in Mali, so the claim is recorded as a limit on one practice, not abolition (B, to verify against Ibn Battuta).
- **Violence of conquest.** No source describes the human cost of the war or the fall of Sosso. The absence of evidence is recorded; nothing is assumed either way.

## Legacy and myths

### Legacy

- **A living tradition.** Sundiata is still celebrated by jeli across the Mande-speaking world, now through radio and television as well as live performance ([Met Museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-empires-of-the-western-sudan-mali-empire), A). Few founders anywhere are remembered this way after eight centuries.
- **The empire he founded.** Mali dominated the western Sudan for generations and became world-famous under Mansa Musa, whose pilgrimage of 1324–25 displayed its wealth to the Islamic world and Europe (Met Museum, A).
- **Modern identity.** Sundiata and the Manden Charter are central symbols in Mali, Guinea and the wider Mande region, and Niane's version of the epic is widely read in schools.
- **For this database,** he is the model case for weighing oral tradition fairly: neither dismissing it nor reading it as a chronicle.

## Learning aids

### Key takeaways

1. Sundiata led a Mande coalition that overthrew Sosso rule and founded the Mali Empire in the 13th century.
2. His life is known mainly through an oral epic still performed by jeli; Arabic writers of the 14th century confirm the core story but drew on the same Mande memory.
3. The broad events are well supported; the dates, the childhood and the magic are tradition, not record.
4. The Manden Charter is a real living tradition, but its exact medieval wording cannot be proven.
5. Mali reached its greatest size and fame after him, under Mansa Musa.

### Compare with

- **Joan of Arc:** both stories hinge on divine or spiritual authority, recorded by very different systems: courtroom notaries versus hereditary oral historians.
- **Pachacuti (Inca):** another empire-builder known largely through traditions recorded long after his death.
- **Te Rauparaha:** the next pilot. Māori oral history and genealogy raise similar questions about weighing tradition against colonial-era written records.

### Pilot notes for the template

- **New claim field needed: `claim_type`** with values `event`, `tradition` and `text`. Without it, "the epic says he could not walk" and "he could not walk" would share one grade, which misrepresents both. This will matter for Pachacuti, Shaka, Kamehameha and every Indigenous and oral-history figure on the list.
- **Battle records must allow `strength: unknown`.** Kirina has no figures, and a required number would push future records toward guessing.
- **`derives_from` worked as intended:** it showed that Ibn Khaldun and Ibn Battuta partly rest on the same oral tradition, so their agreement is weaker than it first appears.
- **Overall grade C was the right result** for a figure whose existence is secure but whose details are not. The weakest-core-claim rule handled this without any special case.
